## [corrected] Spec §7.2.1 asserts Rothermel is biased in chaparral in a single, uncontrolled direction, and the open question asks for "the documented direction and magnitude of Rothermel's bias in chaparral" on the premise that "the literature widely states it underpredicts".

**Correct value:** There is no single signed bias. The sign of Rothermel's error in chaparral is set by the FUEL MODEL, not by the spread equation. The only published field validation of Rothermel in Californian chaparral gives BOTH signs on the same fire, same equation: with the operational reverse-fitted shrub model (FBPS fuel model 4) it runs HIGH; with a physically inventoried custom chamise fuel model (CHAMISE2) it runs LOW by ~4x. Ship this as the headline statement and delete any unqualified "Rothermel underpredicts chaparral" claim.

## [confirmed] Magnitude of Rothermel + FM4 bias at field scale in chaparral (unknown in spec).

**Correct value:** OVER-prediction by ~1.25x. On the Bee Fire (9,620 ac / 3,848 ha chamise chaparral, San Bernardino NF, 29 Jun - 2 Jul 1996; 28 spread events reconstructed from successive perimeters; observed ROS 3-265 ch/hr = 1-89 m/min): "observed spread rates were about 80 percent of those predicted using FBPS fuel model 4". Regression OROS = 25.4 + 0.82*(FBPS4), R^2 = 0.60; intercept not significantly different from zero at alpha=0.05, slope significant, so the unit-independent result is the slope 0.82. Caveats to carry: n=1 fire, a "heat wave" fire not a Santa Ana fire, and 4 of 28 events (polygons 2, 7, 18, 22 — all with generally downslope wind) had influential residuals and were not fit by the regression.

**Citation:** Weise et al. 1997, Bee Fire, Abstract p.114; Table 2 p.117; Eq. 3 and text p.119. https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/53320.pdf

## [confirmed] Magnitude of Rothermel bias when driven by a physically measured chaparral fuel model (unknown in spec).

**Correct value:** UNDER-prediction by ~3.8x. Same fire, same equation, custom chamise fuel model CHAMISE2 (Weise 1997): "observed spread rates were ... about 380 percent of those predicted using the custom fuel model CHAMISE2". Regression OROS = 31.5 + 3.79*(CHAMISE2). Predicted range 2-57 ch/hr for CHAMISE2 vs 9-274 ch/hr for FBPS4 against observed 3-265 ch/hr — i.e. the custom model cannot reach the observed upper range at all. The authors note "the only difference between rate of spread predictions for the two fuel models appears to be a matter of scale, even though the fuel model parameters were quite different."

**Citation:** Weise et al. 1997, Bee Fire, Abstract p.114; Eq. 2 and text pp.118-119. https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/53320.pdf

## [confirmed] Quantified laboratory failure of Rothermel in live chaparral (spec says only "failing to predict spread at all in nearly all fires").

**Correct value:** 240 laboratory fires in high-bulk-density live chaparral beds (chamise, ceanothus, manzanita, scrub oak; North Mountain Experimental Area, CA, 2003-2006). Of 123 fires that actually spread, Rothermel at default settings predicted spread in exactly 1 (chamise: predicted-no/actual-yes = 69, predicted-yes/actual-yes = 1; broadleaf: 53 and 0). Classification AUC 0.507 (chamise) and 0.500 (broadleaf) = indistinguishable from chance. Error measures (Table 7): mean bias -0.15 (broadleaf), -0.23 (chamise), -0.19 m/min (all); MAE identical to |MB| and NMAE 0.98-1.00, i.e. predicted ROS was effectively zero; FAC2 (fraction within a factor of two) = 0.00-0.01. Pagni by contrast: FAC2 0.86, NMAE 0.43-0.49. CRITICAL CAVEAT the spec must carry: the authors themselves state this dataset "is limited in its applicability to field-scale wildland fire spread. With their higher bulk density, these fuel beds are more akin to forest litter fuel beds". Do not quote this as the field-scale bias.

**Citation:** Weise et al. 2016, IJWF 25:980-994. Table 5 p.987 (classification), Table 7 p.988 (FAC2/MB/MAE/NMAE/RMSE), Discussion p.989 (applicability caveat). https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/52537.pdf

## [corrected] Whether raising the live moisture of extinction fixes Rothermel in chaparral (implied by spec's framing of live Mx as the core defect).

**Correct value:** No. Raising live extinction moisture (Rothermel2) and the Cohen, Wilson and Catchpole moisture modifications all improved classification (AUC 0.53-0.69, spread correctly predicted 49-69% of the time) but not to usable accuracy, and the authors conclude: "their similarity of performance suggests that factors other than moisture of extinction are influencing the performance (or lack thereof) of the Rothermel model in these live fuel beds." Only the physically based models (Pagni, Balbi) fell within a factor of two of actual rates.

**Citation:** Weise et al. 2016, IJWF 25:980-994, Abstract p.980 and Discussion p.989. https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/52537.pdf

## [confirmed] Whether a better freely documented chaparral-specific ROS model exists (candidates named in the question: Rothermel & Philpot, Cohen's FIRECAST, Albini, Riverside Fire Lab, USDA PSW/RMRS).

**Correct value:** NO shippable one exists in free form. Each candidate was obtained and rejected on a specific ground: (a) COHEN's FIRECAST (GTR PSW-90, 1986) is free and complete as a manual, but it is not a chaparral model — its ROS engine is "The Rothermel fire spread model (Rothermel 1972) with modifications by Albini (1976a, b)" (sec. 2.1, p.1) driven by two SCAL brush fuel models, SCAL-B "Mixed chaparral (nonchamise)" and SCAL-C "Chamise" (sec. 3.5, pp.4-5), whose parameters come from Rothermel & Philpot 1973. (b) ROTHERMEL & PHILPOT 1973 (J. Forestry 71(10):640-643) is the source of those parameters and is paywalled at Oxford Academic; no free equivalent printing of the SCAL coefficients was located. Note FIRECAST's own caution, p.6: "recent research indicates that age is not a reliable indicator of chaparral fuel characteristics." (c) LINDENMUTH & DAVIS 1973 (RM-101) is free and prints both model versions in full, but it is Arizona SHRUB LIVE OAK chaparral, is fitted to 32 fires of single-clump to 1/4-acre size, deliberately excludes slope ("Slope was intentionally eliminated from the study by locating all plots on level or practically level ground", p.5), and requires net solar radiation and foliar phosphate phosphorus as inputs — unusable in a simulator. (d) RIVERSIDE FIRE LAB (RFL) custom chaparral fuel models (Weise & Regelbrugge 1997) are free in tabulated form — RFL 15 old chamise: 1-h 4.48, 10-h 6.73, 100-h 2.24, live herb 1.12, live woody 4.48 Mg/ha, depth 91.44 cm; RFL 16 ceanothus: 5.04 / 10.76 / 4.04 / 6.73 / 6.28 Mg/ha, depth 182.88 cm — but the free table gives NO SAV and NO moisture of extinction, so the models cannot be instantiated, and the closely related CHAMISE2 is the model that under-predicted 3.8x on the Bee Fire. (e) ALBINI & ANDERSON 1981/1982 "Predicting fire behavior in US Mediterranean ecosystems" is a symposium paper, not located free.

## [confirmed] Independent evidence on Rothermel's performance in chaparral from a second, non-Californian chaparral dataset.

**Correct value:** Rothermel with a constant moisture of extinction "accounts for only 12 percent of the variation in the research data"; the NFDRS variant accounts for 21 percent; "The latest version includes a variable moisture of extinction which does not work in Arizona oak chaparral." The paper also documents a SEASONAL bias direction that is directly relevant to a simulator with a weather model: "Contemporary models that give heavy weight to wind velocity, dead fuel moisture, and fine fuel moisture ... predict an ROS that is too high for the cool half of the year (November-April) and too low for the warm half (May-October) in Arizona oak chaparral." Also documents a wind threshold: "Wind is a limiting factor; a velocity of at least 7 to 8 m.p.h. is needed for fuel to burn well" (3.1-3.6 m/s), and a linear no-spotting wind response of ~4 in/min (20 ft/hr) per additional mph at 20 ft.

**Citation:** Lindenmuth & Davis 1973, RM-101, model comparison pp.3-4; Discussion p.8; Wind Velocity findings p.7. https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/33437.pdf

## [confirmed] Whether substituting SH5/SH7 for FM4 makes the chaparral prediction better or worse (the crux of "defensible rather than merely convenient").

**Correct value:** BETTER, and the argument is citable in one line. Scott & Burgan's own crosswalk from the original 13 to the new set lists BOTH SH5 and SH7 as replacements for original fuel model 4 "Chaparral", each "For slightly lower spread rate and flame length". Combined with the Bee Fire result that observed ROS was ~80% of the FM4 prediction, moving FM4 -> SH5/SH7 shifts the prediction DOWN, i.e. toward the observed value. Additionally SH5/SH7 carry dead M_x = 15% versus FM4's 20%, so they stop carrying fire at a lower dead-fuel moisture — more conservative in marginal conditions, which matches the marginal-burning behaviour documented for chaparral prescribed fire. Their packing ratios (0.00206, 0.00344) also fall inside the measured chamise range 0.00068-0.00374, which FM4's does not by construction.

**Citation:** Scott & Burgan 2005, RMRS-GTR-153, shrub-type crosswalk table pp.13-14 (SH5 and SH7 rows, column "4 Chaparral"); M_x from per-model pages 45 and 47. ## [confirmed] What is specifically wrong with representing chaparral through SH5/SH7 (the honest limit to publish).

**Correct value:** The substitution works by moving the live crown into the dead category. SH5 dead load / total load = 5.70/8.60 = 66%; SH7 = 11.00/14.40 = 76%. Measured mature chamise dead fraction is 24.4% by weight (mean of 16 shrubs; range 5.1-37.7%). So SH5/SH7 carry 2.7-3.1x the measured dead fraction. Consequences to state in the HUD and export metadata: (i) only 34% (SH5) / 24% (SH7) of the fuel load responds to live fuel moisture at all, so the model's sensitivity to LFM is roughly a third of physical reality and its sensitivity to RH / dead fuel moisture is correspondingly exaggerated — this is precisely why the LFM gate must be applied OUTSIDE the Rothermel call; (ii) both SH5 and SH7 are STATIC fuel models (fuel model type "N/A", not "dynamic"), so no live-to-dead transfer occurs with curing or senescence, and the seasonal/age-driven change in chaparral dead fraction cannot be represented within the fuel model — it must be represented by switching SH5 <-> SH7 or by an explicit dead-fraction field; (iii) Scott & Burgan built the new set by behaviour-fitting, not fuel inventory — "we adjusted the parameters of many draft fuel models to better coordinate fire behavior outputs of related fuel models" — so SH5/SH7 are not measurements of chaparral and must never be exported as such.

**Citation:** Scott & Burgan 2005, RMRS-GTR-153, Table 7 pp.17-18 (loads, fuel model type column); development method p.3. Countryman, C.M.; Philpot, C.W. 1970. Physical characteristics of chamise as a wildland fuel. USDA FS Res. Paper PSW-66, Amount and Distribution of Dead Fuel p.6 and Table 4 p.6. https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/28638.pdf

## [confirmed] Heat content assumed by SH5/SH7 versus measured chamise (a quantifiable, correctable bias).

**Correct value:** SH5/SH7 assume h = 8,000 BTU/lb (18.6 MJ/kg). Measured chamise by bomb calorimetry (6 shrubs): foliage 8,968; <1/4 in 8,995; 1/4-1/2 in 8,327; 1/2-1 in 8,393 BTU/lb — i.e. 20.9 / 20.9 / 19.4 / 19.5 MJ/kg. The fine fuel that carries the front is therefore ~12.4% higher in heat content than assumed (8,995/8,000). Because Rothermel reaction intensity is linear in h, the shipped configuration under-states ROS by ~11-12% from this term alone, all else equal. This is the one bias in the whole substitution that can be corrected exactly and with a citation: set fine-fuel h = 9,000 BTU/lb (20.9 MJ/kg) for chaparral, or leave it at 8,000 and state the -11% in the HUD. Cause: ether extractives (waxes, oils, terpenes, fats) are 8-12% of foliage weight and 3.4-8.8% of woody weight, with extract heat values up to 17,378 BTU/lb (foliage) and 24,533 BTU/lb (wood).

**Citation:** Countryman & Philpot 1970, PSW-66, Heat Content p.10 and Table 5 p.10; Chemical Composition p.10 (citing Philpot 1969a) for extractives. Scott & Burgan 2005, RMRS-GTR-153, Table 7 pp.17-18 for the 8000 BTU/lb assumption.

## [confirmed] Spec §7.2.3: "Dennison & Moritz (2009) found a robust threshold near LFM = 79 % in southern Californian chaparral below which large fires occur."

**Correct value:** Confirmed, with the precision the spec is missing. Species: chamise (Adenostoma fasciculatum) only, sampled by the Los Angeles County Fire Department at 13 sites, 1981-2006, per Countryman & Dean (1979, PSW-RP-259) methodology, interpolated to daily resolution. "Large fire" = >=1000 ha. Piecewise regression on cumulative area burned vs LFM gave breakpoints of 79.0, 77.2, 78.4 and 76.2% for fires whose centroids lay within 5, 10, 15 and 20 km of an LFM site — "a breakpoint for LFM exists in the relatively narrow 76-79% range"; 79% was adopted as the maximum of these. Fits below the breakpoint were much stronger (multiple R^2 = 0.92-0.96) than above (0.68-0.76). 29 large fires qualified; the closest interpolated LFM at which a large fire occurred ranged from a MAXIMUM of 78.5% down to a MINIMUM of 53.2%. The paper explicitly does not bracket the threshold's uncertainty: "The uncertainty bracketing the 79% threshold could not be [estimated] ... the 79% threshold should still be a useful guideline." So implement the sigmoid centred at 79% but justify the ~10% width as a modelling choice, not as a published confidence interval.

## [corrected] Spec §7.2.3 asserts chaparral must be represented as "one 2D shrub layer" because it burns as a single crowning layer; the open question asks whether this can be represented at all inside a Rothermel surface formulation and what specifically will be wrong.

**Correct value:** The premise is half right and the correction matters for the 3D module. Chaparral fire is a DUAL-layer crown fire, not a single layer: it "typically ignites in a dead surface fuel layer and transitions to an elevated live crown layer where it continues to spread", and the explicit indictment of the shipped approach is in the same abstract: "Despite the relative importance of surface-to-crown transition and crown fire spread, most fire models represent chaparral fire as surface fire, therefore omitting key behavior processes driving this fire system." Measured magnitudes of what is lost, in a low-velocity wind tunnel with a live chamise crown over a dead excelsior surface layer, wind 1 m/s vs none, crown base height 0.6 vs 0.7 m: surface ROS 2.70 / 1.70 / 1.12 / 0.91 cm/s against crown ROS 3.58 / 2.45 / 1.08 / 1.03 cm/s. So under wind the crown layer spreads ~33% (CBH 0.6 m) to ~44% (CBH 0.7 m) faster than the surface layer, while with no wind crown and surface rates are equal within noise. Peak heat release rate rose from 328 to 526 kW (CBH 0.6 m) and 243 to 503 kW (CBH 0.7 m) when wind was added. Concrete consequences of the Rothermel-surface shipping choice, to publish as the honest limit: (1) no surface-to-crown transition criterion exists, so the sharp go/no-go behaviour of chaparral is replaced by a continuous response and must be reinstated externally by the LFM gate plus a wind threshold; (2) head ROS under wind will be low by roughly a third relative to a true crowning layer, PARTIALLY OFFSETTING the ~25% FM4-family over-prediction rather than adding to it — do not double-count these; (3) crown base height, the variable that measurably controls transition and heat release, has no representation in Rothermel at all; (4) fireline intensity fed to the canopy/firebrand modules is a surface-layer intensity and will understate the crown-layer heat release.

**Citation:** Cobian-Iniguez, J.; Aminfar, A.H.; Saha, S.; Awayan, K.; Weise, D.R.; Princevac, M. 2022. The transition and spread of a chaparral crown fire: Insights from laboratory scale wind tunnel experiments. Journal of Combustion 2022:5630594, doi:10.1155/2022/5630594. Abstract p.1; Introduction p.2; results table of surface vs crown rate of spread and Figure 6 (rate of spread behaviour in surface and crown fires), pp.8-10. Free PDF: https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/65059.pdf

## [confirmed] Landscape-scale evidence on the sign of the error when Rothermel is run over real chaparral fires with chaparral-tuned fuel models.

**Correct value:** Over-prediction of burned area, though confounded by suppression. HFire and FARSITE, both driven by the Rothermel equation with the Riverside Fire Lab chaparral fuel models (NFFL 4 -> RFL 16 ceanothus, NFFL 6 -> RFL 15 mature chamise, NFFL 5 -> RFL 18 coastal sage scrub), simulated the Day, Calabasas and Simi fires in southern California: "Both the Day and Calabasas fires were modeled as being much larger than the actual fires. Justification for that exact word: (a) it is a generic dry-climate shrub fuel model standing in for a chaparral-specific model that does not exist in free form — every named candidate (Rothermel & Philpot's SCAL models, Cohen's FIRECAST, Albini & Anderson, the Riverside Fire Lab custom models, Lindenmuth & Davis RM-101) was obtained or chased and rejected for a stated reason, recorded in the findings; (b) it cannot be called *calibrated* because there is exactly one published field validation of Rothermel in Californian chaparral (Weise et al. 1997, Bee Fire, n = 28 spread events on a single non-Santa-Ana fire, R² = 0.60, 4 of 28 events unfit) — one fire is not a calibration set; (c) SH5/SH7 are behaviour-fitted, not inventory-derived, by their authors' own account, so they carry no measurement provenance of their own.

KNOWN BIAS TO SURFACE IN THE HUD AND EXPORT METADATA — state all four, signed, with the offsets not double-counted:
1. Fuel-model term, +20 to +25 % (runs FAST). Observed ROS was ~80 % of the FM4 prediction on the Bee Fire (slope 0.82). SH5/SH7 are the documented replacements for FM4 "for slightly lower spread rate and flame length", so the shipped configuration should land at or a little above observed. Magnitude rests on a single fire — quote it as "the only published field validation", never as a general figure.
2. Heat-content term, −11 to −12 % (runs SLOW). SH5/SH7 assume 8,000 BTU/lb; measured chamise fine fuel is 8,968–8,995 BTU/lb (20.9 MJ/kg) because of 8–12 % ether extractives. Either set fine-fuel h = 9,000 BTU/lb for the chaparral biome with the Countryman & Philpot citation attached, or leave 8,000 and publish the −11 %. Prefer the correction: it is exact, cited, and one line of code.
3. Missing-crown-layer term, roughly −30 % under wind (runs SLOW). Measured chaparral crown-layer ROS exceeds surface-layer ROS by ~33–44 % under 1 m/s wind and is equal to it with no wind. A Rothermel surface formulation has no crown layer, no surface-to-crown transition criterion and no crown base height, so it loses this entirely. It partially cancels bias 1; say so explicitly so a downstream reader does not stack them.
4. Live-fuel-moisture insensitivity, structural and uncorrectable inside the fuel model. SH5/SH7 place 66 % / 76 % of their load in the dead category against a measured mature-chamise dead fraction of 24.4 %, so only about a third of the fuel responds to LFM at all, and both models are STATIC so no live-to-dead curing transfer occurs. This is why the LFM behaviour must be imposed outside the Rothermel call, and it is the single largest qualitative distortion.

REQUIRED COMPENSATIONS, all now fully sourced:
- Keep the LFM gate at 79 %, now double-sourced: Dennison & Moritz (2009) piecewise breakpoints 76.2–79.0 % across four distance criteria, and Drucker et al. (2023) independently optimising the South Coast bioregion threshold to exactly 79 %. Label the sigmoid's ~10 % width as a modelling choice — the source explicitly declines to bracket the threshold's uncertainty. Do not use 79 % outside southern California; the free per-region optima span 70–98 %.
- Add an explicit wind go/no-go floor. Chaparral does not burn well below roughly 3.1–3.6 m/s in the one free chaparral ROS study that measured it, and wind was the single most important variable for spread success in 240 laboratory chaparral fires.
- Replace the §7.2.3 stand-parameter table's literature composite with the measured Countryman & Philpot (1970) values, and disambiguate bulk density: stand-level (0.51–2.80 kg m⁻³, derived from measured packing ratio × particle density) versus individual-crown (2.43–7.63 kg m⁻³, Li et al. 2017). Those differ by up to an order of magnitude and the crown-fire literature uses the second.
- Correct the two numeric errors found in §7.2.1 and §7.2.3: the Weise et al. (2016) correlations (Pagni/Koo is r = 0.62/0.68, not 0.74/0.89; variants span 0.04–0.49) and the chamise LFM cycle (58–60 % → 105–125 %, not 55–60 % → 120–140 %). Mark the manzanita/ceanothus LFM row UNCONFIRMED for southern California — the only free measured manzanita climatology is for the Klamath and Sierra Nevada, giving minima of 83–84 %, not 60–70 %.
- Kill the unqualified claim that Rothermel under-predicts in chaparral. It is true only for laboratory live-fuel beds (where the authors themselves warn the data are "limited in applicability to field-scale wildland fire spread") and for physically inventoried custom chamise fuel models (−3.8× on the Bee Fire). With the operational shrub models the field-scale error is the other way.
